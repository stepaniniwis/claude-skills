# Digitising the Count, Not the Vote: Evidence from a Live Deployment of Digital Ballot Capture in the 2026 Munich Municipal Election

**Working draft for journal submission, v0.1**

*Author information, affiliations, and acknowledgements to be added. See `submission-notes.md` for open decisions.*

---

## Abstract

Complex preferential ballots make manual vote counting slow and error-prone, yet constitutional constraints in Germany rule out electronic voting. This paper evaluates a third option that has received little empirical attention: digital ballot capture, in which poll workers read paper ballots and record voter marks in software, while the paper ballot remains the authoritative record. We analyse the first legally binding deployment of such a system (Wahllokalerfassungssystem, WES) in a major German city: 23 postal-voting districts in the 2026 Munich municipal election, counted alongside 667 conventionally counted districts. We combine administrative process data, official correction records, and two poll worker surveys (n = 102 WES workers; n = 3,198 general workers). Recording time per city council ballot fell from roughly three minutes under manual counting to roughly one minute. None of the 23 digitally counted districts required a post-hoc correction to its count certificate, against 5.2% of conventionally counted districts; given only 23 treated districts, this difference is directionally consistent but not statistically significant (Fisher's exact p = .30), and we present it as such. Poll worker acceptance was high: 89% reported coping very well with the system, and every respondent who answered the question would use it again. Workers without training coped nearly as well as trained workers, which suggests low operational barriers to scaling. The bottleneck was not capture but integration: offline USB-based provisioning and manual data transfer delayed final results by two days. We discuss what these findings imply for election administration under complex electoral rules, and why digital ballot capture deserves separate treatment from electronic voting in both research and regulation.

**Keywords:** election administration; vote counting; digital ballot capture; poll workers; local elections; Germany; electoral technology

## 1. Introduction

On the evening of a Bavarian municipal election, a single ballot paper can carry more decisions than an entire national election ballot. Munich voters electing their city council distribute up to 80 votes across more than 1,000 candidates, cumulating up to three votes on a single candidate and splitting votes across party lists. Every one of these marks must be identified, validated, and tallied by hand. The counting of the 2020 Munich city council election took days. Manual counting under these rules is not a clerical formality; it is the most labour-intensive, error-prone step of the entire electoral process.

The scholarly and regulatory debate about technology in elections has largely been a debate about voting machines and internet voting, and it has produced a well-founded scepticism. Direct recording electronic machines failed constitutional review in Germany (Taylor, 2010), were abandoned in the Netherlands (Jacobs & Pieters, 2009), and ballot marking devices in the United States have been shown to break the audit chain between voter intent and recorded vote (Appel et al., 2020). Cross-national evidence associates electronic tabulation of results with lower, not higher, electoral integrity (Haque & Carroll, 2020). The German Federal Constitutional Court's 2009 judgment made the principle of the public nature of elections binding doctrine: every essential step of an election must be verifiable by ordinary citizens without special expertise (Taylor, 2010; Volkamer, 2010). Electronic voting in Germany has been politically dead ever since (Fitzpatrick & Jöst, 2022).

This debate, however, obscures a category difference. Between hand counting and machine voting lies a third arrangement: poll workers count paper ballots publicly, as before, but record the marks they read into software instead of onto tally sheets. The paper ballot remains the authoritative record and can be recounted at any time; what changes is the recording and aggregation layer. German research groups anticipated this arrangement more than a decade ago, arguing that electronic *counting* support for complex ballots could satisfy the constitutional publicity requirement that electronic *voting* cannot (Volkamer, 2010; Budurushi et al., 2014). Yet the empirical base for this middle path is thin. Budurushi et al. (2014) evaluated a prototype in a university election. The United Kingdom's e-counting deployments used scanners rather than human capture and were evaluated mainly as procurement and transparency failures (Kitcat & Browning, 2008; Lock et al., 2008). To our knowledge, no study has evaluated a legally binding deployment of human-operated digital ballot capture in a major German election, with administrative outcome data and systematic poll worker surveys.

This paper provides that evaluation. In the March 2026 municipal election, the City of Munich deployed a digital ballot capture system (Wahllokalerfassungssystem, WES) in 23 of 690 postal-voting districts as a proof of concept, covering the city council and district committee elections while the mayoral election remained fully manual. We ask three questions. First, what does digital capture do to the speed and staffing of the count? Second, what does it do to counting quality, measured by official post-hoc corrections to count certificates? Third, do the volunteer poll workers who must operate such systems accept them, and how much training do they need?

We answer these questions with three data sources: administrative process and outcome data from the city's internal evaluation, the official correction statistics for all 690 postal-voting districts, and two post-election poll worker surveys (n = 102 WES workers, n = 3,198 workers overall). The design is observational and the pilot districts were not randomly assigned, so we frame all between-group comparisons as descriptive. Where the underlying counts permit a test, we report one, including where it weakens the headline result: the zero-correction record of the WES districts, set against 5.2% of conventional districts, does not reach statistical significance with only 23 treated districts (Fisher's exact p = .30). The efficiency and acceptance findings do not depend on between-group inference and are correspondingly more robust: recording time per ballot fell by roughly two thirds, and acceptance among poll workers was near-unanimous, with a 95% confidence interval for renewed-use intention of [96%, 100%].

The contribution is twofold. Empirically, we provide the first outcome evaluation of legally binding digital ballot capture in a German metropolitan election, on all three dimensions that matter for adoption decisions: efficiency, quality, and workforce acceptance. Conceptually, we argue that digital ballot capture is a distinct object of study that the literature's e-voting frame mishandles. The risks that condemned voting machines (an unverifiable link between voter action and stored vote) are structurally absent when the paper ballot remains authoritative and the capture step is public. The risks that remain (provisioning logistics, data integration, input error under time pressure) are different in kind, mundane rather than constitutional, and our results show they are the binding constraints in practice.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 describes the institutional setting: Bavarian municipal electoral rules, the German constitutional frame, and the WES system. Section 3 situates the study in the literatures on vote counting accuracy, e-counting deployments, and poll workers. Section 4 describes the pilot design and data. Section 5 reports results on efficiency, quality, and acceptance. Section 6 discusses implications and limitations. Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Setting: Counting Complex Ballots Under a Strict Constitutional Frame

### 2.1 The counting problem in Bavarian municipal elections

Bavarian municipal electoral law gives voters as many votes as there are seats to fill. For the Munich city council this means 80 votes; for the 25 district committees (Bezirksausschüsse), further votes on separate ballots. Voters may cumulate up to three votes on a single candidate (kumulieren) and distribute votes across candidates from different party lists (panaschieren). A single valid city council ballot can therefore encode dozens of individually attributable candidate votes, marked in idiosyncratic combinations, including a list cross whose remaining votes must be imputed to the list's candidates in order.

Counting such ballots manually requires each ballot to be read aloud, interpreted, tallied per candidate, and cross-checked. Munich's election office estimates roughly three minutes of processing time per city council ballot under the conventional procedure. The count of a municipal election routinely extends into the following day, and the personnel requirement is severe: the 2026 election required several thousand poll workers for postal-vote counting alone. Recruitment for municipal elections is harder than for other elections precisely because prospective workers anticipate the counting burden; several survey respondents in our data volunteered this point unprompted.

The scale of the error surface follows from the same complexity. When a count certificate (Wahlniederschrift) is found during official review to contain arithmetic or transcription errors, the district's result must be formally corrected. These corrections are logged, which provides a rare administrative measure of counting quality to which we return in Section 5.

### 2.2 The constitutional frame: publicity, not paper nostalgia

The German Federal Constitutional Court's 2009 judgment on voting computers derived from the Basic Law a principle of the public nature of elections: all essential steps of the electoral process must be open to citizen scrutiny without special technical knowledge (Taylor, 2010). The Nedap machines used in the 2005 federal election failed this test because the decisive events (storage and tallying of votes) happened invisibly in memory. The judgment did not ban technology from elections; it banned arrangements in which the authoritative record of the vote is inaccessible to lay verification. Scholars connected to the German secure-voting community read the judgment early on as leaving room for electronic support of the *count* of paper ballots, precisely because the paper remains the verifiable, authoritative record (Volkamer, 2010; Neumann et al., 2013; Budurushi et al., 2014).

Digital ballot capture instantiates this reading. In the arrangement Munich piloted, ballots are counted in public session by the appointed electoral board, as the law requires. Two workers handle each ballot: one reads the marks aloud, the other enters them into the capture software, which validates the entry against the electoral rules (vote budget, cumulation limit, list imputation) in real time. The software's output feeds the count certificate; the paper ballots are sealed and retained. Any doubt about the electronic record can be resolved by recounting paper, and any citizen present observes the same public reading of ballots as under manual counting. The system therefore does not inherit the constitutional defect of voting machines, though it does inherit every operational question about reliability, usability, and process integration. Those questions are empirical, and they are the subject of this paper.

### 2.3 The WES pilot

The system piloted in Munich (Wahllokalerfassungssystem, WES, supplied by a German vendor and previously used in Bremen and Nuremberg) was deployed in a deliberately constrained configuration. The city selected 23 of its 690 postal-voting districts, all counted centrally at a single venue. Each electoral board of eight to nine members worked with three laptops running WES from bootable USB sticks, fully offline: 108 sticks were prepared manually (92 planned, 16 reserve). Captured results were merged across the three laptops via USB stick, transferred to the central election management system on an encrypted stick, and the count certificate was generated from the merged data. The mayoral election was counted manually in parallel in the same districts, which kept a conventional anchor process in every board.

Ahead of the election, 203 poll workers received a 3.5-hour training in 18 sessions, combining the standard counting curriculum with hands-on capture practice on 150 specimen ballots (the conventional training uses 50). Trainers with live WES experience from Bremen supported the deployment on site. The pilot's declared objectives were to test technical feasibility, efficiency gains, error rates, legal soundness, and user acceptance.

## 3. Related Work

### 3.1 Manual counting is not the gold standard it is assumed to be

A recurring assumption in the technology-sceptical position is that hand counting of paper is accurate and that technology introduces error. The empirical record does not support the first half of this assumption. In controlled experiments on post-election hand counting, error rates were substantial even under redundant procedures explicitly designed to catch mistakes, and they varied with counting procedure and ballot type (Goggin et al., 2012). Recount studies in the United States find systematic discrepancies between original counts and recounts even for simple plurality ballots (Ansolabehere et al., 2018). Practitioner assessments conclude that accurately hand-counting large volumes is close to infeasible at scale. These findings concern ballots far simpler than a Bavarian municipal ballot. If hand counting of single-choice ballots produces measurable error, counting 80 cumulable and splittable votes per ballot multiplies the opportunities for mistakes, which is consistent with the 5.2% certificate correction rate we observe in Munich's conventionally counted districts.

### 3.2 E-counting deployments: cautionary tales from a different design

The best-documented European deployments of counting technology are the Scottish and English e-counting elections of 2007, which used optical scanning. The evaluations are sobering: rushed procurement, inadequate testing, design errors that spoiled large numbers of ballots, and in one case a spreadsheet fault that changed a regional result (Kitcat & Browning, 2008; Lock et al., 2008). Two features distinguish those deployments from the arrangement studied here. First, scanning removes the human reading of the ballot and thereby the public legibility of the decisive interpretation step; observers watch machines, not counting. Second, the 2007 failures were concentrated in integration and project governance rather than in the counting concept itself. Both points inform the design Munich chose: human reading with software recording preserves public interpretation, and the pilot's binding constraint turned out, as in Scotland, to lie in integration logistics rather than capture (Section 5.4). Cross-national panel evidence reinforces the caution: electronic result tabulation correlates with lower expert-rated electoral integrity across countries (Haque & Carroll, 2020). Any positive evaluation of counting technology must therefore show more than speed; it must show that quality and verifiability survive.

### 3.3 Poll workers as the human infrastructure of counting technology

Whatever a counting system's technical properties, volunteer poll workers operate it. A substantial literature establishes that poll worker performance shapes both election quality and voter confidence (Hall et al., 2007, 2009; Burden & Milyo, 2015), that training design matters for performance (Glaser et al., 2015; Jerhov et al., 2023; James & Clark, 2023), and that anxieties about older workers' capacity to absorb new election technology are largely unfounded (DeMora et al., 2022). Poll workers are usefully understood as street-level bureaucrats who implement electoral policy with limited oversight (Favreau et al., 2016; Hostetter et al., 2025); their acceptance of a new counting instrument is not a soft outcome but a hard feasibility constraint, because municipal elections depend on volunteers who can refuse to return. Retention research shows that the experience of being effective and supported drives willingness to serve again (Stein et al., 2024). A counting technology that volunteers experience as a burden would therefore carry a hidden staffing cost even if it were technically flawless; one they experience as relief could ease the recruitment problem that complex elections aggravate. Our acceptance results speak directly to this mechanism.

### 3.4 The gap

Prior work thus offers prototype evaluations of capture systems for complex German ballots in non-binding settings (Budurushi et al., 2014), outcome evaluations of scanner-based e-counting in comparatively simple settings (Kitcat & Browning, 2008; Lock et al., 2008), and a rich poll worker literature that has rarely intersected with counting technology. What is missing is evidence from a binding deployment of human-operated digital capture under maximally complex electoral rules, evaluated on efficiency, quality, and workforce acceptance together. The Munich pilot supplies exactly this configuration.

## 4. Data and Method

### 4.1 Design

The study is an observational evaluation of a non-randomised pilot. The city selected the 23 WES districts; poll workers were assigned to WES boards partly on the basis of willingness to be trained on the system. Both facts create selection risks that we address explicitly: WES boards may have been staffed by more motivated or more technology-comfortable workers, and the pilot received atypical attention (experienced supporting staff, a dedicated venue configuration). We therefore treat all comparisons between WES and conventional districts as descriptive, use the official correction statistics of all 690 postal-voting districts as context rather than as a causal control group, and report inferential statistics only to discipline our own claims, not to assert treatment effects.

### 4.2 Data sources

**Administrative process data.** The city's internal evaluation recorded per-step processing times for the WES process (capture, merge, certificate, closing tasks), staffing configurations, and the timeline of result availability. These figures rest on structured observation during the count and on the system's own throughput, not on an independent time-and-motion study; we flag the implications in Section 6.3.

**Official correction records.** For every postal-voting district, the electoral office logs whether the count certificate required a formal correction during official review. We obtained the counts for the 23 WES districts, for a comparison block of 27 conventionally counted districts processed by the same review team (districts 1950–1999), and for all 667 conventionally counted postal-voting districts, including a breakdown by counting venue.

**Poll worker surveys.** Two standardised online surveys were fielded after the election through the city's poll worker administration. The WES survey covered all workers deployed on WES boards; 102 responses are analysable against 203 trained workers, a response rate of roughly 50% (the exact denominator of deployed workers differs slightly from trained workers). Items covered training participation and evaluation, coping with the system, usability, certificate creation, renewed-use intention, and support quality, with free-text fields throughout. The general survey of all poll workers (n = 3,198 analysable responses) shares several support and organisation items verbatim, which permits direct comparison on those items. Item non-response varies by question; we report the answering n per item. Respondents self-selected into the surveys, and satisfied workers may respond at different rates than dissatisfied ones; the direction of this bias is unknown and we discuss it in Section 6.3.

### 4.3 Analysis

Given the design, our analysis is deliberately plain. We report distributions with exact fractions and, for headline proportions, 95% Wilson confidence intervals. For the correction-rate comparison we report one-sided Fisher exact tests, which is the strongest inference the district-level counts allow. Free-text responses were read in full and coded informally for recurring themes; we use them to interpret the closed-ended results and to surface failure modes, not as an independent qualitative study.

## 5. Results

### 5.1 Efficiency: the count compresses from days into an evening

Recording a city council ballot in WES took approximately 60 seconds (up to 100 seconds for boards still developing routine), against approximately three minutes under manual counting: a reduction of roughly two thirds in the dominant cost driver of the count. An electoral board of eight to nine members, organised into three two-person capture teams, processed roughly 400 ballots in three hours, a sustained throughput of about 60 ballots per team-hour. Fixed process costs came on top: 13 to 15 minutes to merge the three laptops' data via USB stick, about 30 minutes for closing tasks including the machine-generated count certificate, and about 45 minutes of setup and post-processing independent of ballot volume.

The compression matters most at the level of the whole count. Conventional counting of the city council election regularly extends into the following day, with an overnight interruption and a resumption late into the next morning. The WES boards realistically completed their districts on election night. Staffing showed a modest saving (eight to nine workers per WES board against roughly ten conventionally), but the report's more consequential observation is the option it creates: either fewer workers per district or more districts per venue with constant staffing. For a city that struggles to recruit municipal-election poll workers, the choice between these options is a policy lever that manual counting does not offer.

Two qualifications belong next to these numbers. The per-ballot times originate from the deployment's own observation rather than from independent measurement, and the fixed costs of the USB-based architecture (provisioning 108 sticks, physical merge and transfer) are excluded from the per-ballot figure and were substantial (Section 5.4).

### 5.2 Quality: zero corrections, honestly framed

None of the 23 WES districts required a correction to its count certificate during official review. The matched comparison block of 27 conventionally counted districts reviewed by the same team had one correction (3.7%). Across all 667 conventionally counted postal-voting districts, 35 certificates required correction (5.2%), with venue-level rates of 9.7% at one counting site and 4.4% at the other. In addition, all 307 ballots that required a formal board decision (beschlussbedürftige Stimmzettel) in the WES districts were, on review, handled without any subsequent correction, and the unreviewed WES results deviated from the final official results by at most 0.3 percentage points for any list.

The pattern is exactly what the system's design predicts. Certificate corrections predominantly arise from arithmetic and transfer errors, and WES eliminates the manual arithmetic and transcription steps in which such errors live; the validation logic also rejects impossible entries (vote budgets exceeded, cumulation limits violated) at the moment of capture. The share of ballots ruled invalid was near-identical in WES and conventional districts, which argues against the alternative explanation that WES boards reached different substantive judgments about ballots.

We resist, however, the temptation to declare the difference statistically established. With 23 treated districts and a 5.2% base rate, the expected number of WES corrections under no effect is about 1.2; observing zero is unsurprising (one-sided Fisher's exact test against all conventional districts: p = .30; against the matched block: p = .54). The city's internal report describes the quality improvement as significant; on the district-level counts, that claim is not yet supportable, and this paper does not make it. What the data support is more modest and still useful: a mechanism-consistent, directionally uniform quality signal across every quality indicator available (corrections, decision ballots, result stability), with the decisive test deferred to a larger deployment. We flag for future deployments that ballot-level error analysis, for instance through double capture of sampled ballots, would deliver far more statistical power than district-level correction counts.

### 5.3 Acceptance and training: near-unanimous, with an instructive nuance

Acceptance among the poll workers who operated the system was, bluntly, as high as a survey of this kind can produce. Of 92 respondents who answered the coping item, 82 (89.1%, 95% CI [81.1, 94.0]) reported getting along with WES "very well" and the remaining 10 "rather well"; no respondent chose a negative category. Usability was rated "very good" by 77.2% and "rather good" by 21.7% (one negative response, n = 92). Among the 42 respondents who had previously counted a municipal election manually and could therefore compare, 41 rated WES "very good" against manual counting. Every respondent who answered the renewed-use item (92 of 92, 95% CI [96, 100]) wanted WES at future municipal elections. Creating the count certificate, a task poll workers dread, was rated "very easy" or "easy" by 94% (81 of 86).

Ceiling effects this strong warrant scepticism, and two features of the data temper it. First, the free-text fields do not read like acquiescence: respondents filed concrete defect reports (an input bug that intermittently inflated vote counts and was cumbersome to reverse; USB merge failures with ambiguously labelled sticks; a certificate printing detour that cost real time) alongside emphatic overall endorsements, frequently in the same response. Several respondents explicitly linked WES to their willingness to serve at future municipal elections, one noting that the counting burden is precisely why recruitment for these elections is difficult. Second, the pattern of complaints is informative: they concentrate on the provisioning and integration shell around the system (USB sticks, merge, printing, a wish for a fourth laptop per board so that no member stands idle), not on the capture concept itself. Workers did not merely tolerate the system; they asked for more of it, and for its rough edges to be filed off.

The training results carry an operationally important nuance. The 3.5-hour training with an extended practical block was rated "very good" or "rather good" as preparation by 99% of trained respondents (84 of 85), and respondents overwhelmingly named the hands-on practice as the most valuable element, with the theory part attracting most of the criticism. Yet the seven respondents who had attended no WES training coped nearly as well with the system as trained workers (6 of 7 "very well", against 89% of trained workers), typically after consulting the printed quick-reference guide. The system, in other words, appears sufficiently self-explanatory that training is insurance and standardisation rather than a hard prerequisite. This finding matters for scaling: rolling WES out city-wide would require training thousands of workers, and the marginal training design (shorter, practice-centred, with online components, as respondents themselves proposed) can apparently be leaner than the pilot's conservative full-length format. This echoes the poll worker literature's consistent finding that hands-on formats dominate lecture formats (Glaser et al., 2015; Jerhov et al., 2023), and it extends that finding to a setting where the technology itself absorbs part of the training burden by constraining errors at entry.

Support structures were rated better by WES workers than by the general poll worker population on identical items: 100% of WES respondents rated the counting-venue support team "very good" or "rather good" against 95% of general respondents, and no WES respondent reported a problem that support could not resolve (against 1.1% in the general survey). We read this less as a property of WES than as a property of pilots: 23 boards at one venue with dedicated, experienced supporters is a support density that a full rollout will not replicate, a point we return to in the discussion.

### 5.4 Operations: the bottleneck is integration, not capture

The deployment's difficulties cluster almost entirely in its offline provisioning architecture. Preparing and configuring 108 bootable USB sticks was labour-intensive; sticks caused occasional failures requiring restarts during the count; the three-laptop merge via physically shuttled sticks was described by workers as the process's most error-prone and irritating step, including one board that was unsure onto which stick its data had been merged. Most consequentially, the complete captured data reached the central election system only on Tuesday evening, two days after the election, although belastbare interim results (within 0.3 percentage points of the final result) existed roughly 30 minutes into the count. The delay was produced entirely by the manual transfer and integration chain, not by capture throughput. The city's evaluation draws the obvious conclusion, an online-provisioned architecture for any scaled deployment, while one free-text respondent supplied the necessary caution from the venue floor: the counting hall's network infrastructure is notoriously weak. The design question for the next iteration is therefore not whether to abandon offline operation (which contributed genuine robustness and independence from local IT) but where on the offline-online spectrum provisioning and result transfer should sit.

Two further operational findings deserve record. Boards with ten members and three laptops left members without tasks, which workers experienced as poor use of their time; the modal concrete improvement request was a fourth capture station per board. And the intermittent input bug reported by several boards (vote counts jumping to implausible values, reversible only laboriously) was caught in every reported instance by the plausibility of the display and the two-person procedure, but it belongs on the defect list ahead of any scaled deployment, because at scale, rare input corruption meets many more opportunities to occur.

## 6. Discussion

### 6.1 What the pilot establishes

Read together, the three result blocks support a specific, bounded claim: for elections whose counting complexity is the binding constraint, human-operated digital ballot capture can compress the count dramatically, is embraced rather than endured by the volunteer workforce, and produces a quality signal that is consistently positive though not yet statistically decisive. The configuration that produced these results is not incidental. Capture preserved the public, human reading of every ballot, which is what keeps the arrangement inside the constitutional frame that ended machine voting in Germany (Taylor, 2010; Budurushi et al., 2014); the paper ballots remained available for recount, which is what separates this design from the tabulation arrangements that correlate with integrity problems cross-nationally (Haque & Carroll, 2020); and the software constrained errors at the point of entry, which is where the complexity of cumulative-and-panachage ballots does its damage under manual counting (cf. Goggin et al., 2012).

The acceptance findings deserve emphasis beyond their headline numbers, because they connect to the staffing economics of complex elections. Municipal elections are the hardest elections to staff precisely because volunteers anticipate the counting burden. If digital capture turns the count from a feared marathon into a task volunteers actively prefer, and our respondents said so in their own words, then the technology's value is not only measured in worker-hours saved but in the easing of a recruitment constraint that the poll worker literature identifies as chronic (Burden & Milyo, 2015; Stein et al., 2024). The finding that untrained workers coped supports the same conclusion from another side: the operational barrier to entry is low.

### 6.2 What the pilot does not establish

The design places hard limits on causal language, and we state them without decoration. Districts were not randomly assigned; WES boards were staffed by workers willing to be trained on a new system; the pilot enjoyed support density and management attention that a routine deployment will not have. Every between-group difference we report (corrections, support ratings, even throughput) is therefore compatible with some degree of selection and Hawthorne effect. The zero-correction result, the pilot's most quotable number, is exactly the kind of result that a 23-district sample can produce by chance, and we have shown that it does not clear conventional significance. Novelty effects may inflate acceptance; systems that delight in a pilot can bore or frustrate at routine scale. And the two-day result delay demonstrates that the deployed architecture, as piloted, would fail the most visible public expectation of a modern count, timely final results, if scaled without redesign.

We also note what the pilot could not observe at all. It ran exclusively on postal-voting districts counted centrally; decentralised counting in hundreds of polling places poses different provisioning, support, and connectivity problems. It covered one election type in one city with one vendor's system. And it measured administrative quality (corrections, deviations), not public perception: whether observers and losing candidates trust a digitally supported count as readily as a manual one is an open question that voter-confidence research suggests should not be assumed away (Hall et al., 2009; Shino et al., 2025), particularly given evidence that experience with a voting method shapes confidence in it (Shino et al., 2025).

### 6.3 Limitations of the evaluation itself

Beyond the design limits, three measurement caveats. The efficiency figures derive from the deployment's structured self-observation, not from independent timing, and the manual-counting baseline of three minutes per ballot is an administrative estimate. The surveys are self-selected with a roughly 50% response rate on the WES side, and item non-response varies; the direction of participation bias is unknown. And the correction statistic, while attractively official, is a coarse district-level proxy for counting accuracy: it captures errors severe enough to force certificate correction and misses ballot-level errors that cancel or fall below review thresholds. A scaled deployment should build ballot-level accuracy measurement (sampled double capture against paper) into its design from the start; it would convert the quality question from suggestive to decidable.

### 6.4 Implications for practice and research

For election administrators, the pilot's transferable lessons are concrete. The capture concept works and is welcomed by the workforce that must carry it; the investment priorities are the integration chain (provisioning, merge, transfer) rather than the capture interface; training can likely be leaner and more practice-centred than a first cautious deployment assumes; equipment ratios (capture stations per board size) determine whether the staffing gain is realised or dissipated in idle time; and interim-result governance needs defined publication rules before the capability tempts anyone into improvisation. For jurisdictions with simpler ballots, the efficiency case weakens, but the standardisation, error-prevention, and auditability arguments retain force; the city's own evaluation reaches the same judgement.

For research, the Munich pilot demonstrates that digital ballot capture is empirically tractable and deserves an evidence base of its own rather than a footnote in the e-voting debate. The obvious next studies are a powered quality evaluation in a scaled deployment, a voter- and observer-side study of confidence in digitally supported counts, and comparative work across the German municipalities now experimenting with similar systems, whose electoral rules differ enough to make complexity a measurable moderator. The theoretical connection to the street-level bureaucracy literature also remains underexploited: capture systems redistribute discretion from tally-sheet arithmetic (where discretion is unwanted) to ballot interpretation (where it is legally intended), and that redistribution is precisely the kind of technologically induced reshaping of street-level work that public administration scholarship is equipped to analyse (Favreau et al., 2016; Hostetter et al., 2025).

## 7. Conclusion

Between the manual count that buckles under complex ballots and the voting machines that constitutional courts rightly stopped lies a narrow but real design space: let humans read paper in public, and let software do the recording and arithmetic. The first binding metropolitan deployment of this arrangement in Germany compressed the dominant cost of a maximally complex count by two thirds, produced no correctable certificate error across 23 districts, and converted its volunteer operators into advocates, while exposing, with equal clarity, that its offline integration chain is not scale-ready and that its quality advantage awaits a deployment large enough to prove it. Both halves of that sentence belong in the record. Digital ballot capture has earned the larger trial that would settle the questions this pilot could only open.
