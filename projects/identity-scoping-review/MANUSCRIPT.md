## Abstract

Research on entrepreneurial identity has grown into one of the most active conversations in entrepreneurship scholarship, unified by a shared premise: that "entrepreneur" names a coherent social category with which individuals identify, and that a single construct — entrepreneurial identity — travels across the settings in which people venture. This review tests that premise. Through a form-sensitive scoping review of 199 studies, we code, for each study, the focal identity *problem* it addresses, the *theory* it applies, and the *outcome* it reports, and we cross-tabulate these against the *form* of entrepreneuring — independent, academic, corporate, family, platform, and social. Three findings follow. First, each non-default context concentrates on a *different* focal identity problem: academic entrepreneurship is overwhelmingly about which of two roles is primary (87% of coded studies), platform work about signalling identity to evaluating audiences (69%), family-embedded venturing about reconciling the venture with prior family identities. Only the historic default — the independent, full-time founder — is internally diffuse, because there "entrepreneur" is treated as an unproblematic category and every kind of identity question is asked at once. Second, theories cluster by context rather than spanning the field. Third, and most consequentially, the one construct that demonstrably *does* travel — Fauchart and Gruber's founder social-identity types, whose measurement scale has been validated across fourteen countries — is nonetheless mobilised to explain *different problems* in different settings: strategic action among independent founders, value reconciliation among social entrepreneurs. We name this distinction **construct portability without problem equivalence**. It reframes the field's much-diagnosed "fragmentation": entrepreneurial identity is a *family of shared instruments* laid over an *umbrella of distinct problems*. The implication for theory is not to build a unified theory of entrepreneurial identity, nor to abandon the construct, but to specify — for any identity claim — the form of entrepreneuring under which it holds.

**Keywords:** entrepreneurial identity, entrepreneurship form, identity work, role identity, scoping review, construct clarity, hybrid entrepreneurship

## 1. Introduction

Consider two people, each described in the literature as doing "entrepreneurial identity work." The first is a university scientist deciding whether to commercialise a discovery. Her difficulty is one of ordering: she is already, securely, a scientist, and the question that consumes her is whether the commercial persona she is acquiring can be kept subordinate to the academic self she does not want to lose (Jain, George, & Maltarich, 2009). The second is a food-delivery courier building a following on a platform. His difficulty is one of visibility: there is no settled prototype of "gig entrepreneur" to step into, and what he can become depends on how legibly he can perform an entrepreneurial self to an algorithm and an audience that decide whether he is seen at all (Alacovska, Bucher, & Fieseler, 2025). Both are filed under the same construct. Yet the scientist's problem — *which role is primary* — and the courier's problem — *how to be seen as an entrepreneur at all* — are not two values of one variable. They are different questions. A theory that answers one is largely silent on the other.

This review asks whether that difference is the exception or the rule — and what it means for a field that has, for two decades, treated "entrepreneurial identity" as a single coherent construct.

**The stakes are conceptual, not merely taxonomic.** Entrepreneurial identity matters to the field because who one takes oneself to be is held to shape what one does: identity guides opportunity evaluation, strategic response to adversity, persistence through failure, and the legitimacy one can claim (Fauchart & Gruber, 2011; Powell & Baker, 2014; Cardon, Wincent, Singh, & Drnovšek, 2009). If "entrepreneurial identity" is a coherent construct, then findings about it should accumulate: a mechanism established among independent founders should, with contextual adjustment, inform our understanding of academic, corporate, or platform entrepreneurs. If instead the label groups distinct phenomena that happen to share a word, then the accumulation is illusory — the field would be summing findings about different objects and reporting the total as knowledge about one. Construct coherence is therefore not a housekeeping concern. It determines whether the field's twenty years of results compound or merely coexist.

**The premise has gone untested against the diversity of entrepreneuring.** The foundational studies that gave the field its measures and models were developed in a particular setting: the independent, full-time, high-technology or start-up founder for whom "entrepreneur" is indeed a relatively clear social category (Fauchart & Gruber, 2011; Murnieks, Mosakowski, & Cardon, 2014; Farmer, Yao, & Kung-McIntyre, 2011). Entrepreneuring, however, routinely happens elsewhere and otherwise: alongside wage employment, inside established firms, within academic careers, on digital platforms, and threaded through family and caregiving obligations (Folta, Delmar, & Wennberg, 2010; Welter, 2011; Petriglieri, Ashford, & Wrzesniewski, 2019). Each of these settings imposes its own identity demands. Whether the construct built in one setting survives transport to the others is an empirical question that prior reviews, for reasons we detail in Section 2, were not designed to answer — because they organised the literature by theoretical paradigm or thematic conversation, but not by the *form* of entrepreneuring in which identity is at stake.

**This review is motivated by an incommensurability, not a gap.** The usual justification for a review — that the literature has grown and needs synthesis — applies here but understates the problem. Prior syntheses have already named the field's condition: fragmentation (Mmbaga, Mathias, Williams, & Cardon, 2020), pluralism without integration (Radu-Lefebvre, Lefebvre, Crosina, & Hytti, 2021), terminological inconsistency (Wagenschwanz, 2021). What they have read as a defect to be organised away, we read as a symptom to be diagnosed. Our claim is that the field's claims may be structurally incommensurable across contexts — not merely contradictory, which better measurement could resolve, but addressed to different problems, which no amount of measurement can reconcile. Testing that claim requires an analytical move the prior reviews did not make: coding each study not only for what it says about identity, but for the *problem* identity poses in the setting studied.

**Research question.** We therefore ask: *Is "entrepreneurial identity" a coherent theoretical construct, or an umbrella term grouping distinct phenomena that require distinct theories?* We decompose this into three empirical sub-questions and one interpretive one:

- **RQ1.** *What focal identity problem does the literature address, and does it vary by form of entrepreneuring?* If the same problem recurs across forms, the construct may be coherent; if different forms pose different problems, it may be an umbrella.
- **RQ2.** *Which identity theories are applied in which forms, and do they converge?* If one theory dominates across forms, that argues for coherence; if theories cluster by form, the field may be studying different phenomena.
- **RQ3.** *Where a common construct is studied across forms, do findings converge or diverge?* This is the decisive test: a coherent construct should replicate; a divergent one is reshaped, not merely moderated, by context.
- **RQ4.** *What follows for the status of "entrepreneurial identity" as a construct?*

**Approach and preview.** We answer these through a form-sensitive scoping review of 199 empirical and conceptual studies (2009–2026), assembled through a saturation search across three bibliographic databases and coded along an extended TCCM scheme (theory, context, characteristics, methodology) augmented with two purpose-built codes: the focal identity *problem* and the *outcome valence* of the identity process. The review's central finding can be stated in advance, because its force lies in a distinction rather than a surprise. We find, first, that the identity problem varies sharply and systematically by form (RQ1); second, that theories cluster by form (RQ2); and third — the pivot of the argument — that even the one construct which unambiguously *does* travel across forms is put to work on *different problems* in each (RQ3). We name this **construct portability without problem equivalence**, and argue in Section 6 that it dissolves the coherence question into a more useful one. "Entrepreneurial identity" is neither a single construct nor a mere label: it is a family of portable *instruments* laid over an umbrella of distinct *problems*. The field is a family at the level of what it measures and an umbrella at the level of what it explains.

The review proceeds as follows. Section 2 positions the review against the three prior syntheses and specifies the analytical move they did not make. Section 3 details the scoping method. Sections 4 and 5 present the findings: the mapping of problems and theories onto forms (Section 4), and the direct convergence–divergence tests (Section 5). Section 6 develops the portability-without-equivalence argument and its consequences for theory, method, and the field's research agenda. Section 7 concludes.

## 2. Prior Reviews and the Analytical Move They Did Not Make

The entrepreneurial identity literature has been reviewed three times in recent years, each review substantial and each, in a different way, leaving the coherence question untouched. Taken together they establish that the field is disunified; what none establishes is *why*, in a sense that would tell us whether the disunity is a defect or a signal.

Radu-Lefebvre, Lefebvre, Crosina, and Hytti (2021), reviewing 153 articles, organise the field around a single master distinction: entrepreneurial identity as *property* — a set of relatively stable psychological attributes studied for its outcomes — versus entrepreneurial identity as *process*, a dynamic emergence studied for its antecedents and construction. Their integrative map runs along antecedents, content, and outcomes, and their diagnosis is that the field "revolves around two primary conceptualizations" that rarely intersect. Mmbaga, Mathias, Williams, and Cardon (2020), through a bibliometric analysis of 180 articles, partition what they call a "largely disjointed body of existing work" into four "conversations" — distinctions, variations, constructions, and intersections — that proceed in relative isolation from one another. Wagenschwanz (2021), reviewing 132 articles under a systematic protocol, diagnoses the field's problem as terminological: "theoretical and terminological inconsistencies have hampered the development of a coherent understanding of the identity of entrepreneurs," and the remedy proposed is conceptual clarity — a disciplined nesting of "entrepreneurial identity" within "founder identity" within the founder's overall self-concept, crossed with the role, social, and personal identity perspectives.

These reviews share an organising principle and, with it, a blind spot. Each sorts the literature by something *internal* to identity theory — its paradigm (property/process), its thematic conversations, or its terminology — and each treats the entrepreneur as, in effect, a single kind of person whose identity can be theorised in general. The *form* of entrepreneuring — whether identity is at stake for an independent founder, an academic spinning out a discovery, an intrapreneur inside a corporation, a mother running a venture from home, a gig worker on a platform, or a social entrepreneur reconciling logics — enters none of the three as a systematic boundary condition on what identity is and does. This is not for want of awareness that context matters. Radu-Lefebvre and colleagues come closest, closing with an explicit call to examine "the interplay between EI and temporal, socio-cognitive, and spatial contexts" and to theorise "overlooked mechanisms of reconstructing and losing EI" — but context here is named as an unrealised future avenue and framed as three abstract dimensions, not as a typology of entrepreneuring forms that could moderate identity's antecedents and outcomes. Mmbaga and colleagues' "variations" conversation catalogues varieties of identity *content* among entrepreneurs and their agenda points toward venture *stage* and *digital* settings, but not toward venture form as a moderator. Wagenschwanz's framework is individual-level throughout; where non-standard settings appear — academic entrepreneurs' delegating and buffering, social entrepreneurs' competing logics — they appear as illustrative identity *content*, not as boundary conditions, and the term "context" is used in the narrow sense of the work/non-work divide.

The gap, stated precisely, is therefore not that prior reviews ignored context — one of them expressly called for it — but that none organises entrepreneurial-identity knowledge *by the form of entrepreneuring as a systematic moderator*, and so none is positioned to ask whether the identity problem itself changes across forms. This is the analytical move the present review makes. By coding each study for the focal identity *problem* it addresses and cross-tabulating that against the form of entrepreneuring, we convert an acknowledged-but-unsystematised call for context into a form-conditioned account of the field — and in doing so, we can put the coherence question to a test that the property/process, four-conversation, and terminological framings were not built to run. Where prior reviews asked how to *organise* a fragmented field, we ask what the fragmentation *is* — and whether it is failure or fidelity.

## 3. Method

We conducted a **form-sensitive scoping review**. The choice of a scoping rather than an aggregative (meta-analytic) or purely narrative archetype follows from the research question. Because the object under examination is the *coherence of a construct* across heterogeneous settings — not the size of an effect, which would presume the construct already coheres — the review must map the conceptual territory and test whether its regions connect, a task for which scoping logic is designed (Kunisch et al., 2023; Hiebl, 2023). The review combines three analytical moves in Kunisch et al.'s (2023) terms: *problematizing* (interrogating the premise of construct coherence), *classifying* (mapping problems and theories onto forms), and *configuring* (comparing outcomes across contextual configurations).

### 3.1 Search strategy

We assembled the corpus through a combined search designed for comprehensiveness across the *forms* of entrepreneuring, since the review's contribution depends on adequate coverage of settings that keyword searches anchored on "entrepreneur" and "founder" systematically under-sample. Three principal approaches were combined (Hiebl, 2023): database keyword search, backward and forward citation tracking, and expert/manual inclusion of anchor studies.

The keyword architecture crossed two blocks. **Block A (identity constructs)** captured the identity side: `identit*`, `"identity work"`, `"role identity"`, `"social identity"`, `"narrative identity"`, `"possible selves"`, `"identity conflict"`, `"identity centrality"`. **Block B (entrepreneuring forms)** was the review's methodological innovation and the corrective to prior work: rather than searching `entrepreneur*` and `founder*` alone, it explicitly enumerated forms — `intrapreneur*`, `"corporate entrepreneur*"`, `"academic entrepreneur*"`, `"hybrid entrepreneur*"`, `mumpreneur*`, `"gig econom*"`, `"platform work*"`, `"self-employ*"`, and others — alongside the conventional terms. A transitional supplement (`"entrepreneurial exit"`, `"venture failure"`, `"serial entrepreneur*"`) captured identity across role entry and exit. Searches were run across Web of Science–indexed content, Scopus-indexed content, and full-text scholarly indexes (via the Scite, Consensus, and Scholar Gateway interfaces).

Because keyword search alone under-samples context-specific work — a pilot confirmed that a canonical platform-identity study (Petriglieri et al., 2019) did not surface through form-neutral terms — the database search was supplemented by systematic backward snowballing from the prior reviews and all included studies, and forward tracking from the field's anchor works (Fauchart & Gruber, 2011; Hoang & Gimeno, 2010; Powell & Baker, 2014).

**Saturation.** Rather than fixing an arbitrary stopping rule, we ran the search to *saturation of angles*: for each form, successive query reformulations (by construct, by theory, by population, by outcome) were run until new formulations ceased to surface includable studies not already captured. Six form-specific search strands (platform, corporate, family, transitional, academic, and a cross-theory strand) were each pursued through 6–15 reformulations to this stopping point. Saturation was reached unevenly and informatively: the platform and family strands saturated with large yields, whereas the corporate/intrapreneurial strand saturated at a genuinely small volume — itself a finding (Section 6.3).

### 3.2 Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Non-content criteria, applied at title and abstract: English-language; peer-reviewed journal articles (with landmark books and chapters admitted by logged manual inclusion); published 2009–2026; empirical or conceptual (teaching cases and practitioner pieces excluded). The window opens in 2009, the year that both marks the consolidation of entrepreneurial identity as a measurable focal construct (Cardon et al., 2009) and the first systematic documentation of a context-specific identity problem — the academic hybrid (Jain et al., 2009); earlier foundational work enters through logged snowballing.

Content criteria, applied at full text: the identity of the *entrepreneuring individual* had to be a focal construct (studies of organisational, venture, or brand identity were excluded); the form of entrepreneuring had to be codable from the sample; identity had to be theorised rather than named in passing; and studies of entrepreneurial *intention* were admitted only where identity, not intention, was the focal construct.

**On quality thresholds.** We deliberately imposed *no journal-ranking cutoff* for inclusion. A ranking threshold would have systematically removed the newer, context-specific outlets in which platform, mumpreneur, and informal-economy identity research appears — and because those are precisely the non-default forms whose divergence from the founder prototype the review exists to detect, a ranking cutoff would have biased the corpus *toward* the appearance of coherence. In place of exclusion we recorded each study's ranking and ran the central finding as a robustness check restricted to higher-ranked outlets (Section 4.1); the pattern holds.

### 3.3 Screening, sample, and transparency

Screening followed an A/B/C logic: clear include (A), uncertain pending second reading or full text (B), clear exclude (C). Title-and-abstract screening yielded 153 clear includes and 65 uncertain (B) studies; the uncertain set was then adjudicated at full text against the content criteria, which excluded 19 (chiefly studies where identity proved organisational, discursive-collective, or a mere control variable rather than the individual's theorised construct; conference abstracts with no journal version; and studies whose focal construct was motivation, legitimacy, or role rather than identity) and left one unresolvable case pending. The procedure yielded a working corpus of **199 in-sample studies** (198 confirmed includes and one still-uncertain), distributed across forms as: independent (90), family (30), platform (26), academic (23), social (14), corporate (10), and a small residual of mixed/other-professional hybrids. Every uncertain decision and its resolution is logged in the supplementary screening record. Because the review's argument turns on cell-level patterns rather than a single pooled estimate, the uneven distribution is reported transparently and interpreted with corresponding caution for the thinnest cells (corporate, social).

### 3.4 Coding scheme

Coding was **deductive** for RQ1 and RQ2, using categories fixed in advance from the pilot, with an abductive escape valve: studies fitting no category were coded `OTHER` with a memo, and any cluster of five or more such memos triggered reconsideration of the scheme and re-checking of prior codes (constant comparison). For each study we extracted the standard TCCM fields (theory, context, characteristics, methodology) and two purpose-built codes.

The **focal identity problem** (RQ1) was coded to one primary of six categories, defined by the question the study's research question and contribution actually address: `ACTION` (how identity guides entrepreneurial action or strategy), `HIERARCHY` (which of several coexisting roles is primary), `PROTOTYPE` (whether a clear category to identify with exists at all), `VALUES` (whether the venture aligns with prior identities and values), `AUDIENCE` (how identity is performed and signalled to evaluating audiences, including algorithms), and `PERSISTENCE` (whether an identity survives a role transition such as entry, exit, or failure). The **theory code** (RQ2) recorded the identity theory applied — social identity/self-categorisation, role identity, narrative identity, possible selves, identity work, impression management, identity conflict, and the Fauchart–Gruber founder-identity-types tradition. The **context** was coded on two dimensions: *locus* (independent, academic, corporate, family, platform, social) and *role structure* (exclusive, hybrid, transitional). For RQ3, an **outcome valence** recorded whether the identity process the study described succeeded/stabilised (S), failed/destabilised (F), or was conditional (C).

Two decisions in the RQ3 procedure guarded against confirmation bias. First, comparisons were built by grouping studies on *construct* similarity and retaining only groups that spanned two or more context cells, so that divergence and convergence had equal opportunity to appear. Second, the synthesis rules mapping the pattern onto a conclusion (coherent construct / family of constructs / umbrella term) were specified *before* the full corpus was assembled, so that the verdict could not be fitted to the data after the fact.

## 4. The Identity Problem Varies by Form

If entrepreneurial identity were a single construct expressed across settings, we would expect the *problem* it poses to be broadly the same wherever entrepreneuring occurs — with context adjusting the intensity, not the substance, of that problem. The corpus shows the opposite. When the 199 studies are cross-tabulated by their focal identity problem and the form of entrepreneuring they examine, each non-default form concentrates on a *different* problem, and only the historic default form is internally diffuse. Table 2 reports the distribution.

| Academic | 23 | 1 | **20** | 2 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | HIERARCHY (87%) |

The pattern is not subtle. **Academic entrepreneurship is a literature about role hierarchy.** In 20 of 23 studies, the identity problem is which of two already-secure roles — scientist and entrepreneur — takes precedence, and how their coexistence is managed without the academic self being displaced (Jain et al., 2009; Lam, 2010; Giunti & Duberley, 2023; Zou et al., 2019). Hardly any academic study asks whether an "entrepreneur" category is available to step into; the scientist already inhabits a secure professional identity, so the question is ordering, not existence. **Platform entrepreneurship is a literature about audience.** In 18 of 26 studies, identity is a matter of performance and signalling — to customers, to followers, and above all to the algorithms that determine visibility and income (Alacovska et al., 2025; Scolere, Pruchniewska, & Duffy, 2018; Bellesia et al., 2019). **Family-embedded and social venturing are literatures about reconciling the venture with a prior identity.** In family entrepreneurship the corpus splits evenly between value alignment — whether the venture can serve motherhood or family obligation (Duberley & Carrigan, 2013; Lewis, Rumens, & Simpson, 2022) — and role priority in succession — which family member is the legitimate entrepreneurial successor; both are versions of the same underlying demand to fit the venture to the family self. In social entrepreneurship the modal problem is value alignment proper: reconciling commercial activity with a social mission (Wry & York, 2017). **Corporate entrepreneurship, though thinly represented, is a literature about the prototype itself** — whether a stable "intrapreneur" identity is even available to be claimed inside an organisation built around other roles (Starmann et al., 2025; Ravenelle, 2019, on the parallel platform case).

Against these concentrated cells stands the independent, full-time founder — the setting of the field's foundational studies — which is the one form with *no* dominant problem. No single problem accounts for more than 30% of its 90 studies; action, persistence, audience, and value questions are all asked there in volume. This is not noise to be cleaned up. It is the signature of the default. Where "entrepreneur" is treated as a clear, unproblematic social category — as it is for the independent founder — the category itself recedes from view and researchers are free to ask every *downstream* identity question at once: how the identity guides action, whether it survives failure, how it is signalled, how it aligns with values. The independent cell is diffuse precisely because it is the cell in which the prior question — *is there a coherent category here at all?* — is never asked. The non-default forms are concentrated because each forces that prior question into a particular, form-specific shape.

**Robustness.** Because the corpus deliberately included lower-ranked outlets (Section 3.2), we re-ran the mapping on the subset published in journals ranked 3, 4, or 4\* on the Academic Journal Guide. The concentration survives: among higher-ranked studies, academic entrepreneurship remains 77% HIERARCHY, platform 70% AUDIENCE, family 53% VALUES, social 57% VALUES, and the independent cell remains diffuse (modal ACTION, 30%). The divergence of problems across forms is therefore not an artefact of admitting newer or lower-status venues; it is present in the field's most selective journals.

### 4.1 Theories cluster by form, and no substantive theory spans the field

The same form-dependence appears when studies are cross-tabulated by the identity *theory* they apply (RQ2). One approach — **identity work**, in the Sveningsson–Alvesson tradition — recurs across every form (independent 30, platform 22, family 15, academic 9). But identity work is a *meta-approach*, a way of studying how people fashion selves under strain, not a theory that predicts a particular outcome; its ubiquity reflects the interpretive turn in the field, not a shared explanatory core. Once the meta-approach is set aside, the *substantive* theories segregate by form. Role identity theory concentrates where the problem is role ordering — independent and academic entrepreneurship (20 and 16 studies). Impression-management and legitimacy theories concentrate where the problem is signalling — platform work (14) and the independent-founder audience literature (9). The Fauchart–Gruber founder-identity-types tradition is confined almost entirely to independent (12) and social (4) settings. Identity-conflict frameworks surface where two secure roles collide — academic entrepreneurship (4). No substantive identity theory is distributed evenly across the forms. Theories, like problems, are form-specific.

This convergence of the two mappings — problems cluster by form, and so do the theories recruited to address them — is the first-order evidence for the review's thesis. But it is not decisive. A skeptic can reasonably reply that different forms simply emphasise different *facets* of one underlying construct, and that a portable theory, properly applied, would knit them together. Section 5 confronts that reply directly, by examining what happens when a single construct is actually carried across forms.

## 5. When a Construct Travels: Convergence of Instruments, Divergence of Problems

Section 4 established that problems and theories cluster by form. The natural objection is that clustering reflects emphasis, not incommensurability: perhaps each form foregrounds a different *facet* of one underlying construct, and a sufficiently portable theory would reunite them. This section tests that objection on its own terms, by tracking what happens when a single identity construct is actually carried across forms. Two comparisons do the work. The first (Section 5.1) takes one construct — the *hybrid* identity of someone who holds an entrepreneurial role alongside a prior one — and asks whether it behaves the same way in the two forms where it is most studied. The second (Section 5.2) takes the field's most portable construct — Fauchart and Gruber's founder social-identity types — and asks what problem it is made to solve wherever it travels. The first comparison shows divergence of *outcome*; the second shows divergence of *problem* beneath convergence of *instrument*. Together they specify what "fragmentation" actually consists of.

### 5.1 The same hybrid, opposite fates: academic integration versus corporate collapse

Academic and corporate entrepreneurship pose, on the surface, the identical identity problem. In both, a person holds a prestigious primary role — scientist, corporate employee — and acquires a secondary, potentially contaminating entrepreneurial one. If entrepreneurial identity were a coherent construct, the management of this dual-role structure should follow a common logic, with context tuning its difficulty. It does not. In the academic form, the hybrid identity is repeatedly shown to hold; in the corporate form, it is shown to collapse. The outcome codes make the contrast stark: among academic-hybrid studies reporting an outcome, the identity integrates or is conditionally managed in every case and fails outright in none, whereas among corporate-hybrid studies the modal outcome is failure.

The academic literature does not merely report integration; it specifies the *mechanisms* that stabilise the hybrid, and — tellingly — it disagrees about which mechanism operates while agreeing that some mechanism does. In Jain, George, and Maltarich's (2009) foundational account, scientists protect "a focal academic self and a secondary commercial persona" through two forms of identity work: **delegating**, which externalises commercial activity to technology-transfer offices, and **buffering**, which mentally reframes the entrepreneurial work so as to "retain the essence of their academic role identity." Integration here is asymmetric and defensive: the academic self stays focal precisely because the contaminating activity is held at arm's length. Bousfiha and Berglund (2025) contest this mechanism even as they confirm the outcome. In their study of academic entrepreneurs, founders explicitly did *not* buffer by delegating; tasks for which they were uniquely qualified — "setting technology roadmaps, doing key sales and partnerships, and securing funding — were seen as natural and appropriate parts of their overall professional identities," and coherence emerged instead from the "material and practical organization of everyday work" through professional micro-transitions, resolving upward into superordinate self-labels such as "problem solver" and "innovator." Lam (2010), studying 734 scientists, finds hybrids to be "the dominant category" and "particularly adept at exploiting the ambiguities of 'boundary work'" — for her, boundary ambiguity is a resource. Giunti and Duberley (2023), across 81 STEM academics in three countries, are the most cautious: they "argue against the idea of fusion," holding that the two roles "coexist, [but] are separate, as the academic identity remains the central salient identity," anchored by "supranational factors such as norms and values of the academic profession." These accounts differ on the route — buffering, material demarcation, boundary exploitation, disciplinary anchoring — but converge on the destination: a hybrid that is liveable because the primary identity has an external institution to stand on.

The corporate case removes that anchor, and the hybrid collapses. Starmann and colleagues' (2025) eighteen-month study of intrapreneurs in an internal corporate-venturing programme reaches a categorical finding: "no participant managed to integrate their employee and entrepreneurial work roles into a coherent identity as intrapreneur." Instead they oscillate "between two more clearly defined identities — the idealized entrepreneur identity and the corporate employee identity," settling into "an unstable middle ground." The authors name the condition the **intrapreneur identity illusion** — "the inability to construct a coherent and positively valued self-concept based on the intrapreneurial role" — and trace it to exactly the anchors the academic case supplies and the corporate case withholds: organisational dependence denies autonomy, the corporate context refuses to *verify* the emergent entrepreneurial self, and — decisively — there exists no independent "intrapreneur" institution, prestige, or role schema on which the hybrid could stand. Intrapreneurship, "defined primarily in relation to entrepreneurship rather than through its own distinct characteristics," offers "neither the prestige of entrepreneurship nor the stability of corporate employment." One informant renders the outcome exactly: being an intrapreneur "is the combination of the bad aspects of both of these worlds."

This is not moderation. A moderator would make the hybrid *harder* in one setting than the other while leaving the phenomenon intact. What the corpus shows is that the same dual-role construct is a different phenomenon in the two forms: in the academic case an identity to be *integrated and protected*, in the corporate case an identity that *cannot form for want of a category to form around*. The academic problem is HIERARCHY (ordering two secure roles); the corporate problem is PROTOTYPE (whether a stable role exists to be claimed at all). One construct name, two problems, opposite outcomes.

**A qualified counter-voice.** The strongest in-corpus argument against this reading is Cesinger and colleagues' (2022) study of sustainable entrepreneurs, which finds hybrid identities that are, in the literature's phrase, "multiple, yet coherent." On inspection the paper qualifies rather than overturns the divergence claim. Its own mechanism is "the ebb and flow of identity" — coherence achieved through "fluid hybrid identity conflict management strategies," in which "identity shifts dependent on the tension" the entrepreneur experiences. This is managed oscillation elevated to a coping style, not the durable fusion the corporate intrapreneurs fail to reach; indeed the "multiple, yet coherent" formulation is offered partly as a characterisation of the prior literature the study extends rather than as its own headline result. Coherence, where it appears, is thus itself context-dependent and processual — which is the review's point, not its refutation.

### 5.2 Portability without equivalence: one instrument, many problems

The dual-role construct diverges in outcome. The field's *most portable* construct diverges in something more fundamental: the problem it is asked to solve. Fauchart and Gruber's (2011) typology of founder social identities — Darwinian, Communitarian, Missionary — is the strongest candidate the field has for a coherent, transportable entrepreneurial-identity construct, and it earns that status. Sieger, Gruber, Fauchart, and Zellweger (2016) converted the qualitative typology into a fifteen-item scale, validated it "in the Alpine region and … 13 additional countries and regions," and released it in sixteen languages. A construct that measures stably across fourteen national contexts is, by any reasonable standard, portable; a flat claim that "no entrepreneurial-identity construct travels" would be false, and the review does not make it.

Yet portability of the instrument does not carry problem-identity with it. Where the typology is applied to independent, commercial founders, it is recruited to explain **action**: Fauchart and Gruber's original founders' identities "systematically shape key decisions in the creation of new firms"; Alsos and colleagues (2016) use the types to predict "whether the individual predominantly engages in effectual or causal behaviour"; Estrada-Cruz and colleagues (2019), across 5,076 founders, use them to explain decision logic as it varies with national culture. Where the *same* typology is carried into social entrepreneurship, it is recruited to explain **values** — the reconciliation of competing social and commercial logics. Wry and York's (2017) identity-based model theorises "how different types of entrepreneurs perceive the tension between these logics"; Żur (2020) finds social entrepreneurs holding "hybrid identities of all three pure identity types" and developing "meta-role identities, acting as intermediaries" to handle "social-business tensions"; Pan and colleagues (2019) argue over which identity theory best captures the "other-oriented behavior at the heart of social entrepreneurship." Same instrument; in one context a predictor of strategic action, in another a lens on value conflict.

The most economical evidence that this is a real distinction and not an artefact of who cites whom comes from the construct's own authors. Gruber and MacMillan (2017), writing programmatically, direct the identical Communitarian–Missionary distinction squarely at the social-venture values problem — "scholars may want to obtain new insights on social entrepreneurs … by distinguishing between those with a Communitarian identity and those with a Missionary identity … because the behaviors of these types of entrepreneurs are guided by different logics and worlds of meaning, they also derive self-worth in fundamentally different ways" — while in the same breath framing the independent reading around "very different economic and social outcomes." The people best positioned to unify the construct instead deploy it on two different problems, because the settings pose two different problems. Portability of the instrument is real; equivalence of the problem is not.

We name this pattern **construct portability without problem equivalence**: a measurement instrument may transport faithfully across forms of entrepreneuring while the identity *question* it is enlisted to answer changes from one form to the next. It is the precise anatomy of what prior reviews diagnosed loosely as "fragmentation." The field is not fragmented because its instruments fail to travel — some travel remarkably well. It is fragmented because the *problems* do not, and a shared instrument pointed at different problems produces bodies of findings that cannot be summed. Section 6 draws out what this means for the coherence question and for the field.

## 6. Discussion

### 6.1 Answering the coherence question

We asked whether "entrepreneurial identity" is a coherent theoretical construct or an umbrella term grouping distinct phenomena (RQ4). The corpus supports neither answer in its simple form, and the reason it supports neither is itself the contribution. Read at the level of *instruments* — the theories, scales, and constructs the field uses — entrepreneurial identity has the features of a coherent research programme: a shared vocabulary, a validated measurement scale that travels across fourteen countries, a common canon of foundational works. Read at the level of *problems* — the identity question actually at stake in a given setting — it has the features of an umbrella: academic entrepreneurship studies role hierarchy, platform work studies audience, family and social venturing study value alignment, corporate venturing studies whether a prototype exists at all, and only the independent founder is asked every question at once. The construct is a family at the level of what it measures and an umbrella at the level of what it explains. This is why prior verdicts of "fragmentation" were accurate as description but unsatisfying as diagnosis: they registered the disunity of problems without distinguishing it from the unity of instruments, and so could recommend only more integration — more organising frameworks laid over material that no framework can integrate, because the incommensurability is in the questions, not the tools.

The practical upshot is a reorientation of what a cumulative science of entrepreneurial identity would require. The implicit programme of the field — visible in calls to reconcile property and process views, or to consolidate terminology — treats cumulation as a matter of connecting instruments. Our findings suggest cumulation instead requires *scoping claims to problems*. A finding that founder identity guides strategic action (an ACTION claim, established among independent founders) does not travel to gig workers not because the construct is invalid there but because the gig worker's identity problem is AUDIENCE, not ACTION; the finding is not wrong in the new setting, it is *addressed to a different question*. Progress comes not from a unified theory of entrepreneurial identity but from specifying, for any identity claim, the form of entrepreneuring — and hence the identity problem — under which it holds.

### 6.2 Fragmentation as signal

This licenses a re-description of the field's condition. Where prior reviews read fragmentation as a pathology to be cured by better organisation, our mapping suggests it is an *adaptive response* to a real heterogeneity in the phenomenon. Researchers studying academic scientists reach for role identity and identity-conflict theory because the problem in front of them is genuinely one of role ordering; researchers studying gig workers reach for impression management and identity work because the problem in front of them is genuinely one of audience and prototype. The theories cluster by form (Section 4.1) because the problems cluster by form, and the problems cluster by form because different ways of entrepreneuring confront people with different identity predicaments. On this reading the field is not failing to integrate; it is correctly tracking a differentiated reality with differentiated tools. The error to be corrected is not the fragmentation but the residual assumption of unity that makes fragmentation look like failure — the assumption that a single "entrepreneurial identity" underlies all of it.

That assumption has an identifiable origin, which the corpus makes visible. The independent, full-time founder is the field's default case, and it is the one form with no dominant problem (Section 4). We can now see why the default was mistaken for the general. In the setting where "entrepreneur" is an available and unproblematic social category, the prior question — *is there a coherent identity here to have?* — never arises, and researchers proceed directly to downstream questions of action, persistence, signalling, and value. The foundational studies, developed in this setting, could therefore treat entrepreneurial identity as a given and study its consequences. Generalised to settings where the category is *not* given — where it must be ordered against a prior role (academic), claimed without a prototype (corporate), performed to an algorithm (platform), or reconciled with motherhood (family) — that treatment imports an assumption that the new settings violate. The field universalised a construct whose coherence was a local property of its original habitat.

### 6.3 A problem typology as scaffolding

If problems rather than instruments are the field's real units, the six focal problems identified here (ACTION, HIERARCHY, PROTOTYPE, VALUES, AUDIENCE, PERSISTENCE) offer a scaffolding the field currently lacks. They are not a new theory of identity; they are a map of the questions identity poses across forms of entrepreneuring, against which any study can locate itself and any claim can be scoped. Two uses follow. First, the typology disciplines generalisation: a reviewer or author can ask whether a mechanism established for one problem is being silently exported to another, which is the most common way that cross-form claims overreach. Second, it exposes *structural* gaps — problem-by-form cells that are thin or empty not because they are unimportant but because the field's default lens never brought them into view. The most conspicuous is the corporate cell, which saturated at just ten studies despite a hard, multi-database search (Section 4). That thinness is not a sampling failure; it is a finding. The intrapreneur's identity problem — the absence of a prototype to claim — is both real (Starmann et al., 2025) and radically under-studied, precisely the combination that marks an opportunity for theory. Notably, it is also the setting closest to the multi-role, partially-committed forms of entrepreneuring that are increasingly common in practice, which suggests the gap is consequential beyond the academy.

### 6.4 Implications for method

The portability-without-equivalence result carries a methodological instruction. Because a validated instrument can travel across forms while the underlying problem changes, measurement invariance is *not* sufficient evidence that a construct means the same thing across settings. A scale that fits equally well among independent and social entrepreneurs (as the Fauchart–Gruber scale does) can nonetheless be answering different identity questions in the two groups, and pooling them will produce estimates that are statistically clean and theoretically hollow. Studies that carry an identity construct into a new form should therefore establish not only that the instrument measures reliably there, but that the *problem* it indexes is the same — a burden the field has not typically recognised, because it has assumed the problem constant. Comparative, cross-form designs that hold the construct fixed and let the problem vary are the natural way to meet this burden, and they are almost entirely absent from the corpus.

### 6.5 Research agenda

Four directions follow specifically from the findings. **First, populate the structural gaps.** The corporate/intrapreneurial cell needs primary work that takes the absence of a prototype as its starting point rather than importing the founder model; the same holds for identity across platform *transitions* and for the under-studied PERSISTENCE problem outside independent founders. **Second, build cross-form comparative designs.** The review's central claims rest on comparison across studies conducted in single forms; the decisive test would hold one construct fixed and compare its problem and outcome across two or more forms within a single design — the academic-versus-corporate hybrid contrast (Section 5.1) is the obvious first candidate, since the divergence there is sharpest and the mechanism best specified. **Third, take the problems, not the construct, as the theoretical object.** Progress on AUDIENCE identity (how entrepreneurs signal a self to algorithms and publics) or on PROTOTYPE identity (how a self forms where no category is available) is unlikely to come from a general theory of entrepreneurial identity and likely to come from theories built for those problems specifically, imported from impression management, occupational-category formation, and the sociology of valuation. **Fourth, test the default-case hypothesis directly.** Our claim that the independent cell is diffuse *because* the category is unproblematic there is an interpretation of a distribution; it could be examined directly by studying settings where the "entrepreneur" category is becoming available or contested and observing whether the prior question re-emerges.

### 6.6 Limitations

Several design choices bound these conclusions. Most of the corpus was coded at the title-and-abstract level; the pivotal studies in Section 5, and the sixty-five initially uncertain cases, were adjudicated at full text, but the remaining cell counts in Section 4 rest on abstract-level coding and would shift somewhat under full-text reading of every study. One uncertain case could not be sourced through any accessible channel and is held pending; its inclusion would not alter any cell's modal problem. The search, though run to saturation of angles across three full-text and citation databases, did not include exhaustive exports from every subscription index, so the review reports a saturation-of-angles sample rather than a closed PRISMA census; the direction we would expect additional retrieval to move the counts — toward more, not fewer, non-default studies — would strengthen rather than weaken the divergence claim, but this remains an expectation. Coding was performed by a single analyst against a pre-registered scheme with an abductive escape valve and logged uncertain decisions; independent double-coding would harden the problem assignments, which are the load-bearing judgements. The thinnest cells (corporate, n = 10; social, n = 14) support directional but not fine-grained claims. Finally, the review is restricted to English-language work, which likely under-represents forms of entrepreneuring salient in non-Anglophone contexts and may itself impose a boundary on the problem typology.

## 7. Conclusion

Entrepreneurial identity research has spent two decades accumulating findings under the assumption that it studies one thing. This review suggests it studies several, held together by shared instruments rather than a shared problem. When the literature is sorted by the form of entrepreneuring in which identity is at stake, the identity *problem* turns out to vary systematically — role hierarchy for academic entrepreneurs, audience for platform workers, value alignment for family and social entrepreneurs, prototype availability for corporate ones — while only the field's default case, the independent founder, is asked every question at once. Even the field's most portable construct, whose scale is validated across fourteen countries, is put to work on different problems in different settings, as its own authors demonstrate. The field is a family at the level of what it measures and an umbrella at the level of what it explains. Naming that difference — construct portability without problem equivalence — does not resolve the coherence question so much as retire it, replacing the unanswerable "is entrepreneurial identity one construct?" with the tractable "under which form of entrepreneuring does this identity claim hold?" Fragmentation, on this account, was never the disease. It was the field, more accurately than it knew, tracking a differentiated reality — and the task ahead is not to integrate the fragments but to specify, honestly, which of them a given claim belongs to.

## Appendix A — Coding scheme summary

**Focal identity problem codes (RQ1).** ACTION (identity guides entrepreneurial action/strategy); HIERARCHY (which of several coexisting roles is primary); PROTOTYPE (whether a clear category to identify with exists at all); VALUES (whether the venture aligns with prior identities/values); AUDIENCE (how identity is performed/signalled to evaluating audiences, incl. algorithms); PERSISTENCE (whether an identity survives a role transition). One primary code per study; a secondary code permitted where a design explicitly addresses two; OTHER with memo where none fit.

**Theory codes (RQ2).** Social identity/self-categorisation (SIT); role identity (RIT); narrative identity (NARR); possible selves (POSS); identity work (IDWORK); impression management/legitimacy (IMPR); identity conflict (CONFLICT); Fauchart–Gruber founder-identity types (FIT); OTHER.

**Context codes.** Locus: independent, academic, corporate, family, platform, social, multiple, other. Role structure: exclusive, hybrid, transitional.

**Outcome valence (RQ3).** S (identity process succeeds/stabilises), F (fails/destabilises), C (conditional/mixed).

**Pre-registered RQ4 decision rules.** (A) coherent construct: one problem primary in ≥5/6 forms and ≥50% of cross-form comparisons convergent; (B) family of constructs: partial adjacent-form overlap of problems and mixed comparisons; (C) umbrella term: no problem primary in >2 forms and majority of comparisons divergent. Observed pattern: rule A fails; the corpus sits between B and C, resolved analytically as portability-without-equivalence (Section 6).
